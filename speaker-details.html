Michele Ceriotti

Hierarchical sampling of the materials universe

Machine-learning models that aim to be transferable across the periodic table require sampling that covers all corners of the compositional and configurational space. To build a training dataset that matches this ambition, we introduce a protocol to stratify a dataset into a hierarchy of nested subsets, subsampling from the coarsest to the finest resolution based on an “ecumetric” built to reflect the latent space representation of several popular universal machine-learning interatomic potentials. We then use this approach to combine most of the open structural datasets currently available, consolidating them into MAD-COSMO, a well-organized collection of almost 1.7 billion structures, whose 224 most diverse members form the MAD-CORE stratified coreset, which can be easily used for all sorts of tasks, from exploratory experiments to the training of foundation models at scale. I will demonstrate the use of MAD-CORE to train usable universal Delta models with as little as 20 thousand structures, and to complement an existing dataset to train PET-MAD-2, a model that provides stable, accurate predictions for any material and molecule from H to No.

I will highlight some research directions related to Anatole von Lilienfeld's work, including models of potential energy surfaces across chemical space. Yousung Jung

Designing Matter That Can Be Made

Artificial intelligence is expanding our ability to explore chemical and materials spaces, but predicting promising candidates is only the beginning. Real-world discovery also requires understanding whether, and how, proposed materials can be synthesized. In this talk, I will present our efforts to connect materials design with synthesis and experimental feasibility. These include generative approaches that use electronic structure as a modality for inorganic materials design, machine-learning models for synthesis prediction and synthesizability assessment, and reaction-prediction methods informed by chemical mechanisms. I will also discuss Materealize, an agentic AI framework, and our broader vision for integrating prediction, synthesis planning, and experimental feedback into autonomous discovery systems.

Speaker Details

Markus Reiher

Automated Reaction Mechanism Exploration Embraces Machine Learning - Or How I Made the Mistake to Not Listen to What Anatole Wanted to Tell Me Years Ago.

The quantum chemical elucidation of complex chemical reaction networks is at the heart of all (computational) reaction chemistry with important applications in catalysis. However, the sheer size of such networks renders manual workflows unfeasible. Instead, automated algorithms are needed which can efficiently explore a vast reaction network with as little human input as possible. Our software project SCINE delivers an open-source and extensible framework for the automated exploration of chemical reaction networks. Due to its stringent first-principles basis, its exploration algorithms avoid any restriction to specific chemical systems. Hence, it can be used for mechanism elucidation, reaction path optimization, retrosynthetic path validation, reagent design, and microkinetic modeling, among other tasks. The automated procedures in SCINE turn quantum chemical reaction exploration into a big data problem. Naturally, machine learning has therefore become pervasive in explorations. In my talk, I will discuss challenges and solutions in this context.

Speaker Details

Mark Tuckerman

Small Chemical Changes with Big Effects: Achieving Breakthrough Performance and Revealing Quantum Effects in Non-Aqueous Electrolytes Through a Narrow Chemical Space Exploration

Candidate systems for next-generation battery electrolyte materials, such as deep eutectic solvents and ionic liquids, often suffer from the limitation of an empirical inverse relation between viscosity and conductivity, known as Walden’s rule, which suppresses rates of charge transport and limits their electrochemical performance characteristics. An alternative to these ionic systems involves a class of systems known as concentrated hydrogen-bonded electrolytes (CoHBEs), which are structured, electrochemically stable and less volatile. CoHBEs can also be designed such that charge transport kinetics and solvent dynamics are largely decoupled in such a way as to break the viscosity-conductivity tradeoff implied by Walden’s rule. The basic strategy for achieving this breakthrough performance is to leverage the Grotthuss transport mechanism by choosing organic molecular solvent species, such as imidazole and organic acids, capable of supporting proton hops through a dynamic, amphoteric hydrogen-bond network. Many combinations exhibit conductivities topping out at around 8 mS/cm, however, we will show that through very simple chemical changes, breakthrough conductivities approaching 60 mS/cm can be achieved, which makes such combinations applicable in redox-flow battery applications. This study, which combines AI-driven simulation with experiment, also reveals a significant role for nuclear quantum effects in driving the system toward the high observed conductivity values.

Tristan Bereau

Chemical compound space at lower resolution

Chemical compound space is usually explored at quantum-mechanical resolution, where every nucleus and electron is resolved. Here we explore the same space at lower resolution. Coarse-grained models built from a small alphabet of bead types map many molecules onto a single parametrization, a degeneracy that drastically reduces the size of chemical space and makes it possible to screen thermodynamic properties across hundreds of thousands of small molecules. We exploit this hierarchy of resolutions to navigate chemical space by multi-level Bayesian optimization, in which coarse models guide the search at finer resolution. Finally, we quantify what is lost on the way down: recasting coarse-graining as measure transport between spaces of different dimensionality yields the mapping entropy, the information discarded by a many-to-one representation.

Speaker Details

Jochen Blumberger

Machine Learning electronic response properties

The atomic polar tensor and the atomic polarizability tensor quantify the change in electric dipole moment and polarizability with respect to a change in nuclear positions. They give access to many important properties of condensed phase systems including ionic conductivities, dielectric constants, vibrational spectra, E-field-driven dynamics and so forth. I review the approach my collaborators and I have taken to machine learning these two tensorial properties and present applications to water, aqueous ionic solutions, solids and interfacial systems.

Daniel Sebastiani

Configurational Entropy of Mixing of Molecular Systems

While the energy is the most basic quantity in molecular simulation, its stepsister entropy is often only discussed on a qualitative level. We present a simple, fast and robust method to compute the configurational entropy of mixing of a multi-component molecular system. Our approach allows to compute the evolution of the entropy of mixing along any trajectory as an explicit time-dependent quantity, enabling the analysis of its explicit kinetics.

Speaker Details

Enrico Tapavicza

In Silico Design of Light-Driven Nanomotors

Light-driven molecular nanomotors hold promise for applications in material science and biomedicine. Significant efforts have focused on improving their efficiency, often targeting single candidate molecules. Here, we present a systematic data-driven approach to design nanomotors with high isomerization quantum yields for one- and two-photon applications, the latter being critical for biomedical applications requiring near-infrared light. We analyze the excited state properties of a dataset of 2016 nanomotors substituted with electron-donating and electron-withdrawing (push-pull) groups. Among the top candidates, we achieved an increase in two-photon absorption strengths of up to two orders of magnitude compared to existing nanomotors. To ensure that the π–π*-character of the excited state is preserved, which is necessary to achieve the required photoisomerization, we introduce a transition density similarity score, that gauges the excited state character based on the transition. Furthermore, we benchmark three machine learning (ML) models—Kernel Ridge Regression, XGBoost, and a Neural Network—using physical and connectivity-based molecular descriptors. The excellent accuracy of our ML predictions holds promise to replace computationally costly quantum chemistry calculations in chemical space explorations.

Speaker Details

John Keith

Better Data, Clearer Mechanisms: DFT-Referenced Coupled Cluster and Quantum Bond Orders Across Chemical Space

Machine learning models for chemistry are only as reliable as their training data, and most large datasets rely on density functional theory (DFT), whose errors are system-dependent and difficult to anticipate. For catalysis and reaction mechanisms, we also want models that offer chemical insight in addition to accurate predictions. I will first show that coupled cluster theory using a Kohn–Sham DFT reference (KS-CCSD(T)) can outperform both DFT and conventional Hartree–Fock-based CCSD(T). In our recent Nature Commun. paper, we showed that this improvement comes from the KS density encoded into the Fock matrix rather than from the KS orbitals themselves. KS references yield near-chemical accuracy for main-group and transition-metal thermochemistry, they recover the notoriously challenging Cr2 potential energy surface, and they enable a low-cost diagnostic for multireference character. Extending this approach with DLPNO-CCSD(T) to bond dissociation energy datasets for inorganic compounds, we find the same trends. I will then turn to interpretability and use quantum-mechanical bond orders that are evaluated across stratified subsets of QM9 and the Transition1x reaction dataset. We show how bond orders can be used to read and interpret reaction mechanisms across chemical space. Finally, I will briefly show progress in a family of bond-order-based semi-empirical models (BEBOP2, ZPE-BOP, and QBOP, with KIE-BOP in development) that connect these descriptors to predictive, physically transparent models.

Speaker Details

Denis Andrienko

Tensorial Molecular Property Predictions with Equivariant GNNs

We propose a first-principles-based parameterization procedure for atomic polarizability tensors and scalars, validated on a set of small organic molecules with conjugated building blocks. To overcome the computational cost of ab initio calculations, we train a message-passing graph neural network to predict polarizability parameters, enabling efficient and scalable parameterization. This approach imposes no additional computational cost during simulations and provides a clear diagnostic criterion for identifying cases where polarizable force-field models fail to accurately describe molecular polarizability.
